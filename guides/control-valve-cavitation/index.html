Control valve cavitation: σ, FL² and choked flow

How to tell whether a liquid control valve will cavitate, flash or choke, using the IEC 60534 factors FL, FF and xFz and the cavitation index σ, with a liquid ammonia example.

By Muhammad Anas, process design engineer · updated 2026-11-07 · version 2026.11.10.1

What happens inside the valve

As liquid passes the plug it accelerates through the smallest flow area, the vena contracta, and its pressure falls well below the outlet pressure before partly recovering downstream. If the pressure at the vena contracta falls below the vapour pressure, vapour bubbles form. If the outlet pressure is above the vapour pressure they then collapse violently: this is cavitation, which pits trim and body and makes noise like gravel. If the outlet pressure is itself below the vapour pressure the bubbles never collapse: this is flashing, and the danger becomes erosion by the high-velocity two-phase jet.

The IEC 60534 factors

FF = 0.96 − 0.28 √(Pv / Pc)    (liquid critical pressure ratio factor)
ΔPchoked = FL² · (P1 − FF·Pv)    (IEC 60534-2-1; FLP/FP replaces FL with reducers)
σ = (P1 − Pv) / ΔP    ·    xF = ΔP / (P1 − Pv) = 1/σ

FL is the valve's pressure recovery factor: a globe valve has a high FL (about 0.9, little recovery), a butterfly a low one (about 0.6, strong recovery, so it cavitates sooner). When ΔP reaches ΔPchoked the flow stops increasing with ΔP: the valve is choked by vapour at the vena contracta. Cavitation starts well before that point; IEC 60534-8-4 gives the incipient-cavitation ratio xFz, and cavitation begins when xF exceeds it.

ConditionMeaningTypical response
xF < xFz (σ high)no cavitationstandard trim
xF ≥ xFz, not chokedincipient to constant cavitationhardened or anti-cavitation trim; check noise
ΔP ≥ ΔPchokedchoked; heavy cavitationmulti-stage trim, or split the ΔP
P2 ≤ Pvflashingangle body, hardened trim, expanded outlet

Worked example: liquid ammonia pump recycle

Duty: liquid ammonia at 25 °C, normal flow 60 000 kg/h, P1 = 21 barg, P2 = 13 barg, 3 inch globe valve (FL ≈ 0.9) in a 4 inch line. Properties: ρ = 602.8 kg/m³, Pv = 10.00 bar a, Pc = 113.3 bar.

Q = 60 000 / 602.8 = 99.5 m³/h  ·  P1 = 22.01 bar a  ·  ΔP = 8.0 bar
FF = 0.96 − 0.28 √(10.00/113.3) = 0.877
ΔPchoked = 10.7 bar (with the reducer correction) → 8.0 < 10.7: not choked
σ = (22.01 − 10.00) / 8.0 = 1.50

The valve is never choked and its travel is comfortable, so on Cv alone it looks fine. But σ is 1.3–1.8 in every case, below the common rule of thumb of 2, so cavitation is likely, worst at minimum flow where the pressure drop is highest. Options: an anti-cavitation (multi-stage) trim; a restriction orifice downstream to take part of the drop; or routing the recycle to a point at higher pressure. The vendor's σ curves for the chosen trim are the final check.

Common mistakes

  • Accepting a valve because it is not choked. Damage starts long before choking.
  • Checking only the normal case. Minimum flow often has the highest ΔP and the worst cavitation.
  • Using FL from a different valve style: a butterfly or ball valve recovers far more pressure than a globe valve.
