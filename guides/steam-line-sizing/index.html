How to size a steam line

Size superheated and saturated steam lines by velocity, pressure-drop gradient and Mach number, with a worked 110 barg / 510 °C header example checked by hand.

By Muhammad Anas, process design engineer · updated 2026-11-07 · version 2026.11.8.6

What governs a steam line

Three things decide the size of a steam line. Velocity drives erosion (especially with any moisture), noise and the force on bends. Pressure drop must fit the pressure available between the boiler or header and the users, and every bar lost is lost superheat and turbine power. At high pressure the friction gradient (bar per 100 m) is usually the binding limit, not velocity. A small line can also approach sonic conditions near a large pressure drop, so the Mach number is checked too.

Method

Steam is compressible, so the line is marched in short steps: at each step the density comes from the steam tables (IAPWS-IF97), the friction factor from Colebrook–White, and the pressure, density and velocity are updated before the next step. For a short line with a small pressure drop this gives almost exactly the same answer as a single step at inlet conditions, which is what the hand check below uses.

G = W / A   ·   v = G / ρ   ·   Re = G·D / μ
ΔP/L = f · (1/D) · ρv²/2    (Darcy–Weisbach, f from Colebrook–White)
ΔPfittings = ΣK · ρv²/2    (K = n·fT, Crane TP-410)

Typical limits

Company standards differ; these are the defaults this calculator uses for superheated steam, and you can edit every one of them.

CriterionDefault limitWhy
Velocity, superheated steam≤ 50 m/serosion, noise, pipe support loads
Friction gradient above 70 barg≤ 0.8 bar/100 mkeeps header pressure and superheat
Mach number≤ 0.3keeps flow well away from choking
Total pressure drop≤ 10 % of inletwarning: check the users still get their pressure

Saturated and wet steam are normally held to lower velocities than superheated steam, because droplets erode bends and fittings.

Worked example: HP steam header

Duty: 100 t/h superheated steam at 110 barg and 510 °C, with a 10 % design margin (110 t/h). Route: 120 m straight carbon steel pipe, six long-radius 90° elbows and two gate valves.

Properties (IAPWS-IF97 / IAPWS 2008): ρ = 33.57 kg/m³, μ = 29.47 µPa·s, saturation temperature 318.8 °C, so the steam has about 190 K of superheat.

Step 1: try 8 inch Sch 160

Inside diameter 173.1 mm. The calculator gives an outlet velocity of 39.9 m/s (inside the 50 m/s limit) but a friction gradient of 2.13 bar/100 m, more than double the 0.8 bar/100 m limit. At this pressure the gradient governs.

Step 2: 10 inch Sch 160

Inside diameter D = 215.84 mm, so A = π/4 · 0.21584² = 0.03659 m².

G = (110 000 / 3600) / 0.03659 = 835.1 kg/m²s
v = 835.1 / 33.57 = 24.9 m/s
Re = 835.1 × 0.21584 / 29.47×10⁻⁶ = 6.1 × 10⁶ (fully turbulent)
speed of sound 650.5 m/s → Mach = 24.9 / 650.5 = 0.038
Result (10" Sch 160)ValueLimitStatus
Velocity, outlet25.1 m/s≤ 50 m/sPASS
Mach number0.039≤ 0.3PASS
Friction gradient at inlet0.677 bar/100 m≤ 0.8 bar/100 mPASS
Total ΔP (120 m + fittings)0.964 bar (0.87 %)≤ 10 %PASS

The Darcy friction factor is 0.0141 (fully rough value fT = 0.0139), and the fittings add ΣK = 1.39, equivalent to about 21 m of pipe. 10 inch Sch 160 is the smallest size that passes. 12 inch would cut the gradient to 0.27 bar/100 m, which is only worth the extra cost if the users are short of pressure.

Common mistakes

  • Sizing on velocity alone. At high pressure the steam is dense, so velocity looks comfortable while the pressure drop is not.
  • Using the nominal pipe size as the diameter. Sch 160 walls are thick: 10 inch Sch 160 has 215.8 mm bore against 254.5 mm for standard wall.
  • Forgetting the design margin, or adding it twice (once on flow and once on the limits).
  • Ignoring fittings on short lines, where they can be a large share of the drop.
